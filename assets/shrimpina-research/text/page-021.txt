Atlantic Sand Fiddler Crab = Leptuca pugilator
    ●​ Description
           ○​ AKA Sand Fiddler Crab
           ○​ Carapace (Shell): a small, squarish carapace, typically around 20–25 mm across
              at most, but its coloration is much more variable than that of the mud fiddler.
              Individuals can range from very dark blue-gray to pale gray or nearly white and
              frequently have a mottled or marbled appearance. A purple patch near the center
              of the upper carapace is a particularly useful identification feature.
           ○​ Lighter Coloration: Compared with the dark mud fiddler, sand fiddlers are
              usually noticeably paler. Their light gray, tan, white, and purple coloration
              blends particularly well into sandy or mixed sand-mud substrates.
           ○​ Male’s Enormous Claw: As with the mud fiddler, males have one enormous
              claw while females possess two small feeding claws. The major claw can actually
              become larger than the rest of the crab’s body, reaching around 35–41 mm in
              especially large individuals.
           ○​ Smooth Claw: One anatomical way to distinguish a male sand fiddler from a mud
              fiddler is the smooth inner surface of the enlarged claw. The corresponding
              surface in closely related mud fiddlers is more strongly textured.
           ○​ Courtship and Competition: Males wave their enlarged claws to attract females
              and use them in contests with neighboring males. Fighting can result in the loss of
              the major claw. If this happens, the crab can regenerate a claw, and the opposite
              side may become the new dominant claw.
           ○​ Sand Specialist: Tend to be found on sandy or mixed sandy-muddy flats,
              particularly along open or near-ocean shorelines and around the margins of salt
              marshes.
           ○​ Burrowing: They excavate burrows in exposed sand and sediment and can occur
              in exceptionally dense colonies. At low tide, large numbers may emerge
              simultaneously to feed across the surface before retreating underground when
              disturbed or as the tide returns.
           ○​ Diet: Sand fiddlers are primarily deposit feeders. They scoop surface sediment
              into the mouth and extract algae, microorganisms, detritus, and other organic
              particles before discarding the cleaned sediment.
           ○​
    ●​ Location
           ○​ Found on exposed sandy areas during low tide. Many toward the sandy outer
              portions of Woodneck.
