Longnose spider crab (Big - Punk rock) = Libinia dubia
   ●​ Description
         ○​ AKA Decorator Crab / Spider Crab
         ○​ Carapace (Shell): has a rounded to somewhat pear-shaped carapace that narrows
             toward the front. Its shell is generally yellowish-brown, tan, or gray-brown,
             although its true coloration can be difficult to see because the surface is often
             covered with algae and other material. The carapace bears several prominent
             spines, including approximately six spines along the midline of the back.
         ○​ The Long “Nose”: One of its most recognizable features is the elongated
             rostrum projecting forward between the eyes. Rather than forming one
             continuous point, the rostrum is forked into two projections, giving the crab the
             long-nosed appearance behind its common name.
         ○​ Spider-Like Legs: Its five pairs of legs are noticeably long, narrow, and jointed,
             making the animal appear almost spider-like as it walks. The first pair terminates
             in relatively small claws, while the remaining legs end in curved tips used for
             walking and gripping the substrate.
         ○​ Slow Movement: Unlike fast-running shore crabs or swimming crabs, the
             longnose spider crab generally relies heavily on camouflage rather than speed. Its
             slow, deliberate movement fits particularly well with a camouflage-based lifestyle
             in which remaining inconspicuous can be more important than quickly escaping.
         ○​ “Velcro” Camouflage: The carapace and portions of the legs are covered in
             specialized hooked setae, or tiny bristle-like structures, that work almost like
             Velcro. The crab deliberately collects material from its surroundings and attaches
             it to these setae, disguising the outline and appearance of its body.
         ○​ Younger Crabs Decorate More: Camouflage through decoration appears to be
             especially important in smaller and younger individuals. Studies have found
             that smaller Libinia have more hooked setae and tend to decorate themselves more
             extensively than larger individuals. As the crab grows, the abundance of these
             hooked structures decreases and its reliance on this particular camouflage strategy
             can change.
   ●​ Location
         ○​ Found deep in the marsh. Walking very slowly alongside a mate or friend about 2
             feet away. After the specimen was placed in a container with a sponge. The crab
             began pulling off small pieces of sponge and attaching them to its own body,
             where the pieces adhered to the Velcro-like setae. This was not simply material
             accidentally sticking to the crab; it is known to actively collect and place available
             material onto its body as decoration. Laboratory studies have found that the
             species will use a variety of available materials, although filamentous algae are
             especially commonly selected in natural environments. In the marsh, the same
             behavior allows the crab to attach algae, plant material, and small organisms to
             itself. Rather than merely matching the color of the environment, these
             decorations also break up the recognizable outline of the crab, making it more
             difficult for predators to distinguish it from the surrounding vegetation and
             substrate.
