    ●​ Description
           ○​ AKA Blue Crab / Blue Claw Crab
           ○​ Carapace (Shell): Has a very broad, flattened carapace much wider than it is
               long. The upper shell is usually olive-green, gray-green, or bluish-green, while the
               underside is pale or white. Along each side of the shell are nine marginal teeth,
               with the final tooth extending outward into a particularly long and sharp lateral
               spine. Giving the crab a distinctly pointed appearance when viewed from above.
           ○​ Bright Blue Claws: One of its most recognizable features is the intense blue
               coloration on the claws and portions of the walking legs. Adult males generally
               have bright blue claw tips, whereas mature females often develop red or
               orange-red tips on their claws.
           ○​ Powerful Claws: Unlike the oversized display claw of a fiddler crab, both claws
               of the blue crab are functional and powerful. Used for capturing and tearing apart
               prey, manipulating food, defending against predators, and fighting other crabs.
               Blue crabs can be highly defensive when handled and will readily raise their
               claws when threatened.
           ○​ Paddle Feet: The final pair of walking legs is flattened into broad, rounded
               paddle-like appendages. These swimming legs distinguish blue crabs and allow
               them to move rapidly through the water.
           ○​ Fast and Agile Movement: Blue crabs are dramatically more mobile than spider
               crabs. Their paddle-shaped hind legs allow them to swim sideways, forward, and
               backward, making them effective both at pursuing prey and escaping predators.
           ○​ Male vs. Female “Apron”: The sex of a blue crab can be identified by examining
               the abdominal flap, or apron, underneath the body. Adult males have a long,
               narrow apron often compared to an inverted T or Washington Monument, while
               immature females have a broader triangular apron. Mature females develop a
               much wider and more rounded apron used to accommodate their egg mass.
           ○​ Marsh and Estuary Habitat: Blue crabs thrive in shallow estuarine
               environments, including muddy bottoms, salt marsh edges, eelgrass beds, and
               other submerged vegetation. These habitats provide both food and protection,
               particularly for juveniles. Southern New England studies have found blue crabs
               occupying shallow tidal rivers in Massachusetts and Rhode Island throughout the
               warmer months.
           ○​ Diet: Blue crabs are aggressive omnivores and scavengers. Their diet can
               include bivalves such as clams and mussels, small fish, worms, other crustaceans,
               aquatic vegetation, and decaying organic matter.
           ○​ A Predator and Prey: Although important predators within estuarine food webs,
               they’re also eaten by larger fish, birds, and other predators, particularly when
               young or immediately after molting.
           ○​ Molting: Like other crabs it must periodically shed its rigid exoskeleton in order
               to grow. Immediately following a molt, the new shell is extremely soft, a stage
               commonly called a soft-shell crab. During this period the crab is particularly
               vulnerable to predators until the new carapace hardens.
